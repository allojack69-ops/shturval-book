The system
CONTOUR

It does not arrive as an enemy. It arrives as an answer to the human desire to make fewer mistakes.

First, the system helps.
Then it predicts.
Then it recommends.
And finally — it defines the limits of what is possible.

The question is no longer only whether you trust the system.

The question is whether you will notice the moment when you stop choosing for yourself.

From the book
The screens in the room had stopped flickering. They simply glowed with a steady, warm white light that dispersed shadows so neatly it was as if shadows were a technical defect.

Beyond the window, the evening megacity hummed in the middle frequencies. Streams of people moved smoothly, without abrupt stops. Almost no one collided at crossings. No one ran on the stairs. Even those who were late were late optimally: their personal algorithms had already recalculated the route, notified the other party, shifted the reservation, the compensation, the meal time, and the recommended load on the heart.

People used to call this planning.

Now they called it background.

— Who decides?

— A committee, statistics, the legal module, Contour, user complaints.

— So no one.

— No. Many people. Don’t confuse distributed responsibility with no responsibility.

Mark looked at the rules.

In old dystopias, someone came at night and changed the words.

In the new system, a rule could update itself through a chain of locally correct procedures.

No liar.

No command.

No single moment you could point to and say: this is where the transformation happened.

That made it harder to analyze.

Not because the system was worse.

Because causality was smeared.
